# The Mom Test — Customer Interview Rules

Apply Rob Fitzpatrick's rules from *The Mom Test* to extract genuine signal from customer conversations, even from people who want to be polite.

## The Core Problem

Customers lie — not out of malice, but out of politeness. When you ask "Would you use this?" or "What do you think of this idea?", they say what they think you want to hear. Even your mom would tell you your startup idea is great.

The solution: **Talk about their life, not your idea.**

## The Three Rules

### Rule 1: Talk about their life, not your idea
- Ask about past behavior, not hypothetical future behavior
- "Tell me about the last time you needed to [do X]" beats "Would you ever want to [X]?"
- Their past decisions reveal real priorities; their future intentions don't

### Rule 2: Ask about specifics, not generalities
- "How do you currently handle this?" → good
- "How often does this come up?" → good
- "Would this be useful?" → bad (hypothetical)
- "Do you think people would pay for this?" → bad (asking for validation)

### Rule 3: Listen, don't pitch
- Pitching triggers social approval mode — they respond to your enthusiasm, not truth
- Shut up after asking. Silence is data.
- If they start talking about your product before you pitch it, that's signal.

## Good vs. Bad Questions

| Bad (Opinion / Hypothetical) | Good (Fact / Behavior) |
|------------------------------|----------------------|
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | "How do you currently solve this?" |
| "Would you pay $X for this?" | "How much do you currently spend on this?" |
| "What features would you want?" | "Walk me through the last time you did this." |
| "Is this a big problem for you?" | "How often does this happen?" |
| "What do you think of our mockup?" | "What would have to be true for you to use this?" |

## The Mom Test Checklist

Before each interview:
- [ ] Have 3 specific behaviors or facts you want to learn
- [ ] No pitching until you've gathered data
- [ ] Questions focus on past/present, never hypothetical future

During the interview:
- [ ] Ask "tell me about the last time..." for every key scenario
- [ ] Follow up specifics: "Who else was involved? What did you do next?"
- [ ] Note exact words customers use — they become your copy
- [ ] Look for **emotional moments** (frustration, excitement) — these signal real pain/desire

## Commitment Over Compliments

A compliment ("This sounds great!") is worthless. Commitment is signal.

**Types of commitment to seek:**
- **Time**: "Can we schedule a follow-up? Can I call you next week to see how it went?"
- **Data**: "Can you share your current spreadsheet / process / examples?"
- **Money**: Pre-orders, letters of intent, deposits
- **Social capital**: "Can you introduce me to two other people who have this problem?"

If a customer won't give any form of commitment, the conversation is noise.

## Conversation Structure

```
1. Framing (2 min)
   "I'm trying to learn about [problem space], not pitch anything.
    There are no wrong answers. I'd love to hear about your experience."

2. Their story (10–15 min)
   "Tell me about the last time you needed to [do the job]."
   Follow specifics: who, what, when, how, what happened next.

3. Current solution (5–10 min)
   "How do you handle it now? What do you like/dislike about that?"
   "How much time/money does this cost you?"

4. Priority (3 min)
   "Of all the problems you've mentioned, which is most painful?"
   "Why hasn't this been solved for you yet?"

5. Commitment ask (2 min)
   Choose one commitment type appropriate to where they are.
```

## Red Flags (Stop and Redirect)

- "I would definitely use this" → Ask: "When was the last time you needed this?"
- "This is exactly what I've been looking for" → Ask: "What have you tried so far?"
- "Everyone has this problem" → Ask: "Tell me about a specific person who has it."
- Lots of feature requests → Ask: "What would you do differently right now if this existed?"
