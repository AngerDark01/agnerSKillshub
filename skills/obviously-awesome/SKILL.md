# Obviously Awesome — Positioning Framework

Apply April Dunford's positioning process from *Obviously Awesome* to transform a product's market position from confusing to compelling.

## Why Positioning Fails

Most companies default to positioning based on how the product was built (features) instead of the value it creates for a specific customer in a specific competitive context. Bad positioning causes:
- Sales friction ("I don't get it")
- Wrong-fit customers churning
- Under-pricing (value not communicated)
- Feature wars instead of category dominance

## The 5-Step Positioning Process

### Step 1: Identify Competitive Alternatives

What do your best customers use if your product disappears tomorrow?

- List all alternatives (not just direct competitors — include spreadsheets, do-nothing, internal builds)
- Focus on what your **best-fit customers** actually use, not the whole market

### Step 2: Isolate Unique Attributes

What can you do that the alternatives cannot?

- List capabilities and features unique to you
- Group related capabilities into **attribute clusters**
- Prune: only keep attributes that matter to customers

### Step 3: Map Attributes to Value Themes

For each unique attribute, ask: "So what? What does this mean for the customer?"

| Unique Attribute | Customer Value |
|------------------|----------------|
| [Feature/capability] | [Business outcome it enables] |

Cluster related value points into **3–5 value themes** — these become your messaging pillars.

### Step 4: Identify Your Best-Fit Customers

Who gets the most value from your unique strengths?

- Look at your happiest, most successful customers — what do they have in common?
- Define **buyer characteristics** (not demographics — firmographics, behaviors, situations)
- Describe the **trigger event** that makes them ready to buy

Best-fit customer profile:
```
Company type: [Size / industry / tech stack / growth stage]
Trigger: [What just happened that makes them need this now]
Decision maker: [Title / role]
Must-have criteria: [Minimum requirements they won't compromise on]
```

### Step 5: Choose a Market Category

The category frames your product's competitive context and sets customer expectations.

**Three strategic options:**

| Option | When to use | Risk |
|--------|-------------|------|
| **Head into existing category** | Category is well understood, you win on key attribute | Must beat established players |
| **Create new category** | No existing category captures your value | High education cost |
| **Reframe existing category** | Existing category exists but wrong frame; you win in subcategory | Must shift mental model |

**Category selection criteria:** Choose the frame where your unique strengths are the most important buying criteria.

```
Value themes:
  1. [Theme name]: [1-sentence value statement]
  2. [Theme name]: [1-sentence value statement]
  3. [Theme name]: [1-sentence value statement]

Best-fit customer:
  [3-5 bullet profile]

Market category:
  [Single phrase — the frame we compete in]

Positioning statement:
  For [best-fit customer], who [trigger/need],
  [Product name] is a [market category]
  that [primary value theme].
  Unlike [primary alternative],
  [Product name] [key differentiator].
```

## Common Mistakes

- **Positioning to the whole market** — positioning is about winning with a specific segment, not everyone
- **Features as differentiators** — competitors will copy features; unique value themes are durable
- **Category before differentiation** — choose the category last, after you know your unique strengths
- **Consensus positioning** — positioning requires a point of view; "something for everyone" is nothing for anyone
